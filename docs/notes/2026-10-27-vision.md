# Pond – Autonomous Communal Urban Farming (27 Oct 2026)

Conversation notes. The vision.

## Vision

- Autonomous communal urban farming for São Paulo: a hybrid of real growing spaces and tech.
- Builds on Dani's COVID hydroponics setup: vertical PVC pipes, lettuce pods, nutrient water pumped on a 15-minute on/off cycle.
- "Espresso capsule" pods: just plug them in.
- A robot arm (3–4 axes plus gripper) on a cart that travels along a modular pipe system (straights and elbows, like Lego).
- The robot monitors plants with a camera, swaps pods, and, as a moonshot, assembles and disassembles the structure itself ("box on the floor, plug in, it builds itself over 3–5 days").
- Solar powered; the robot docks and charges when idle, which is most of the time.
- Bigger dream: urban off-grid living as a product (sink shredder → anaerobic biodigester → biogas for cooking; digestate as fertiliser). Later phase, because of gas safety and regulation.
- Five-year arc: Pond becomes Dani's main occupation.

## Business model

- Open-source designs, so anyone can build the robot-free or robot version; paid tiers for convenience (like Prusa).
- The robot is mainly the brand and the magic; it also teaches people how to grow and gives people back the power of making food.
- Customers: households, schools, councils/communities, green-economy grants and projects.
- Goal: profitable without subsidies, with local manufacturing in Brazil (jobs). Not a dealbreaker if it isn't reached at first.
- Motors and electronics imported (Brazil's WEG makes mainly large industrial motors); casings, pipes and parts made locally.
- Recurring revenue from pods and nutrients (Nespresso-style); a swap-and-recycle service for worn robot parts.

## Hardware notes

- Arm: SO-101 (open source, Hugging Face LeRobot, Python). It uses 6 smart servos, which report their position back.
- Motors: around 5–6 total (arm, gripper, 1 for the cart).
- Design everything around one standard gripper interface (like a tool changer).
- Pipes: standard PVC or polypropylene for the straight sections (cheap, food-safe options). 3D print only the joints, elbows, cart and pod holders.
- Print in PETG or ASA, not PLA (heat/UV). ASA is best outdoors.
- Joints designed for robots: bayonet/twist-lock or chamfered snap-fit rather than screw threads. Joints flush with the pipe surface.
- Cart: spring-loaded wheels clamping round the pipe. Reserve one "no-wheel zone" for supports and brackets. Add a geared strip only if it slips when wet. Avoid tracks; keep plain tubing.
- Durability: IP65/67 sealed servos, shaft lip seals, magnetic couplings through sealed walls, breather vents and desiccant, conformal coating. A dry sealed dock/"garage" for the robot. Sealed motors cost roughly 2–4x hobby servos; not prohibitive.

## Feasibility

- Hobby prototype: very feasible.
- Product: feasible but tough. The main issues are durability over 1–2 years and service costs.
- Full self-assembly plus off-grid home: decade-long north star.

## Open challenges

1. Cart stability on the pipe and handling elbows (crack this first).
2. Self-assembly (moonshot).
3. Pod and nutrient supply chain.
4. Field service logistics in São Paulo (easier at community sites).
5. Retention after the novelty wears off.
6. Public sites: vandalism/theft and food safety rules.
7. Import taxes on electronics.
8. Fleet software (remote monitoring/updates). Dani is confident here.
