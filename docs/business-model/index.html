Our mission

We have lost the know-how to grow our own food, and we have become fully dependent on supermarket chains. Yet growing food is easy to learn and easy to teach. Pond is a quiet, almost sneaky way to get people learning without stopping their day: just by watching the robot plant, tend and harvest, you already start learning how it's done.

Pond's main mission is to empower people to learn how to grow their own food.

A note on where we are: this page is early, very high-level, preemptive thinking. Pond is an open-source project nowhere near going to market. Nothing is for sale, and none of the numbers below are promises.

Pond is a community and educational project first. It will always strongly support community projects, and it will never drop the community arm, however strong the commercial side becomes. If the commercial side grows, it will be in a position to invest in the community arm.

Where Pond fits

Four ways to grow lettuce and greens

1. Big mechanised farms

Huge fields of a single crop, grown with machines. Very cheap per head, but far from the people who eat it.

2. Small family farms near cities

Fresh produce sold at farmers' markets. Lovely, but small and limited by land and labour.

3. Indoor hydroponic vertical farms

A big trend, but many famous companies (such as Infarm, AppHarvest and Bowery) closed or went bankrupt. Their energy and running costs were higher than what the crop earned.

4. Pond

Outdoor, solar-powered, communal hydroponic units looked after by a small robot.

Pond avoids the main thing that sank vertical farms. It runs on sunlight and a solar panel, so energy is close to free.

Pond doesn't try to beat big farms on wholesale cost. It competes with the supermarket price, because it skips transport, middlemen and waste.

Rough economics

The numbers, roughly

Early guesses, not promises

These are back-of-the-envelope figures from our current parts list and cart design (4 motors, omni wheels, arm, battery, solar panel, pipe and tanks). Pond is nowhere near going to market, nothing is for sale, and none of this is a promise.

What a unit might cost

  • Target: about $250–350 per unit including the robot cart, if parts are bought in bulk and made in production runs. The big items are the battery, solar panel and charger (about $120–150), the arm servos and cart motors (about $70–90), the small computer and electronics (about $60–80) and the printed parts, pipe and tanks (about $40–60). We assume it lasts 5 years.
  • Pessimistic: about $500 per unit, lasting 3 years. Today we buy single parts at hobby prices, so a build now costs even more than this.

How much lettuce, and where

  • About 25 plants per unit.
  • Somewhere warm all year (for example Rio): about 10 harvests a year, so about 250 heads.
  • Outdoors in a temperate climate (for example the UK): maybe 4 harvests in the growing season, so about 100 heads.

Cost per head

Hardware spread over its lifetime, plus about 5 cents a head for seeds and nutrients.

  • Target, Rio: $50–70 a year of hardware, so about 25–35 cents a head.
  • Target, UK: about 55–75 cents a head.
  • Pessimistic, Rio: about $167 a year, so about 70–75 cents a head.
  • Pessimistic, UK: about $1.70 a head, which is too dear. Pond would only make sense in a cool climate if the target case comes true.

Not yet counted: mailing the pods, and a person delivering and assembling each unit the first time.

Who it's for

Households, schools and community projects

Pond is for people growing their own food, not for people going into the lettuce business. So the useful question is what a unit is worth to a household or a community garden.

An example in Rio

Supermarket lettuce (for example at Prezunic) is roughly R$4–10 a head, about $0.75–2. At about 250 heads a year, a unit replaces roughly R$1,000–2,500 of shopping (about $190–500).

A unit's yearly cost is about $60–85 in the target case (hardware over 5 years plus seeds and nutrients) and about $180 in the pessimistic case. A subscription that covers upkeep would sit somewhere around that yearly cost. We have not set a price.

Some people might one day want several units to sell produce locally. That only makes sense if bulk pricing brings units well down and they last many years. It is not our focus.

Business model

Two sides, one mission

This is our current thinking, not a plan.

  1. Start with the community side. Raise money from green funds, grants, councils and mayors to give free or subsidised units to community and educational projects, with workshops teaching people how to grow. The robot means people can learn without having to be fully committed.
  2. In parallel, offer a subscription on the open market. Each commercial unit must pay for its own maintenance, so the project never depends on commercial sales.
  3. Let the commercial side prove itself. If it shows it has legs, Pond can become a commercial project with a community arm. Either way, the mission and the community arm stay.
